There were twenty-seven people in the room. I could smell blood. Somewhere under the swarm of scrubs, a spine was being assessed for damage — and I stood at the edge of it all, a volunteer in blue, trying to figure out where I belonged. I had four hours, once or twice a week, to witness what medicine actually looks like. It looked nothing like the version I had imagined.

I did not arrive at medicine with certainty. In community college, I considered dentistry — until I realized I wanted a field that felt less like an ending and more like a beginning. When I was accepted to UC San Diego for neuroscience, I gave myself permission to have no fixed vision of a future. I simply followed what pulled me: the science of the brain, the mystery of how thought becomes biology.

For the next two years, I spent most of my waking hours in the lab. I loved it. I learned to pipette, to run assays, to contribute to published research on mind-body interventions. But slowly, I noticed something I could not unsee: the moments I remembered most vividly were not at the bench. They were with the patients whose samples I was collecting — people living with cancer, PTSD, ALS, MS. I would sit with them before drawing blood, explain the consent form, and somewhere in that exchange, the science receded and the person appeared.

Still, I was on the fence. The world of healing science was expanding rapidly — meditation, breathwork, mind-body practices were producing measurable biological changes. A part of me wondered: if people are learning to access their own capacity for healing, is becoming a doctor the most impactful path forward?

I carried that question into a meditation retreat in Florida, in the thick heat of late summer. It was early afternoon, after lunch. I was sitting on a single yellow couch, the kind of worn, sun-faded piece of furniture that has held a hundred conversations before yours. To my right sat another young woman wrestling with the same uncertainty about medicine. To my left was Kevin Crowe, a retreat leader whose voice was always soft yet solid — a man of few words in moments like this, and somehow all of them were enough.

When the other woman voiced her doubt, Kevin did not offer reassurance. He offered clarity. "We will always need doctors," he said. "The difference you can bring to the field is to become a doctor that stays in her heart and stays present."

I did not need to think about it. I did not need to weigh it. In that hot room, on that yellow couch, something that had been loose in me for years finally locked into place. That was it. That was all I needed to hear.

After that afternoon, I stopped waiting for certainty and started building it. I sought out more patient-facing experiences — not to check boxes, but to test whether that clarity would hold up against the reality of a hospital floor.

It did. But it also demanded more of me than I expected.

As a Care Extender at UCLA, I have four hours, once or twice a week, to be inside the machinery of medicine. In that short window, I cycle through more emotional ground than most people traverse in a month. I have felt the silent weight of a family receiving news that their loved one is not coming home. I have stood beside a patient fresh from surgery, still searching for a hand to hold. I have sat with an anxious, angry patient who believed they were being held hostage — and I have learned that anger in a hospital bed is almost never about what it appears to be.

What I am learning, shift by shift, is that the difference between a doctor who survives this work and a doctor who transforms through it is not resilience in the way we usually mean it. It is one thing to pretend to be fine with all that happens. It is something else to train the heart and the brain to stay focused — and to radiate love through everything that unfolds around you.

Kevin was right. We will always need doctors. But we will always need doctors who stay in their hearts.

I do not yet know what specialty I will pursue. I do know the kind of doctor I am training to become. She will not pretend the chaos does not affect her. She will practice presence the way a surgeon practices suturing — deliberately, repeatedly, with the understanding that mastery is a lifetime project, not a credential. She will remember that the patient who is angry is probably terrified, that the family receiving bad news needs someone who can sit in the silence without flinching, and that the volunteer standing at the edge of the trauma room is often the most important person in it — because she is still learning to see the whole person, not just the case.

I was that volunteer. I am still that volunteer, in the best sense. And I am applying to medical school not because I believe I am ready, but because I believe I am willing — willing to train my heart as rigorously as my mind, willing to stay present when presence is the hardest thing to offer, and willing to become the kind of doctor Kevin Crowe described on a yellow couch in Florida, in a room so hot it could break you, except it didn't. It made me.
