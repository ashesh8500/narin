There were twenty-seven people in the room. I could smell blood. Somewhere under the swarm of scrubs, a spine was being assessed for damage — and I stood at the edge of it all, a volunteer in blue, trying to figure out where I belonged. I had four hours, once or twice a week, to witness what medicine actually looks like. It looked nothing like the version I had imagined.

I did not arrive at medicine with certainty. In community college, I considered dentistry, then realized I wanted a career that felt like a beginning, not an ending. When I was accepted to UC San Diego for neuroscience, I gave myself permission to have no fixed vision, and simply followed what pulled me: the brain, the mystery of thought becoming biology.

For two years, I lived in the lab. I loved it. I learned to pipette, to run assays, to contribute to published research on mind-body interventions. But I noticed something I could not unsee: the moments I remembered most vividly were not at the bench. They were with the patients whose samples I was collecting — people living with cancer, PTSD, ALS, MS. I would sit with them before collecting saliva and cheek swabs, explain how their samples would contribute to mind-body intervention studies, and somewhere in that exchange, the science receded and the person appeared.

Still, I was on the fence. The world of healing science was expanding rapidly — meditation, breathwork, mind-body practices were producing measurable biological changes. A part of me wondered: if people are learning to access their own capacity for healing, is becoming a doctor the most impactful path forward?

I carried that question into a meditation retreat in Florida, in the thick heat of late summer. It was early afternoon, after lunch. I sat on a single yellow couch that had held a hundred conversations. To my right, another young woman wrestling with the same uncertainty. To my left, Kevin Crowe, head of operations for the research we were conducting there, whose voice was always soft yet solid — a man of few words, and somehow all of them were enough.

When she voiced her doubt, Kevin offered not reassurance but clarity. "We will always need doctors," he said. "The difference you can bring to the field is to become a doctor that stays in her heart and stays present."

I did not need to think or weigh it. In that hot room, on that yellow couch, something that had been loose in me for years finally locked into place. That was it. That was all I needed to hear.

After that afternoon, I stopped waiting for certainty and started building it. I sought out more patient-facing experiences — not to check boxes, but to test whether that clarity would hold up against the reality of a hospital floor.

It did. But it also demanded more of me than I expected.

Before I ever stepped into a hospital, I spent twenty months coordinating the STARS program, matching undergraduates with PhD mentors and building systems that connected students to research they could not access alone. On paper, the work was logistical — applications, orientations, seminars — but the instinct was the same one I would later bring to patient care. I learned to listen across hierarchies, to translate between faculty expectations and student needs, and to build structures that outlasted any single coordinator.

As a Care Extender at UCLA, I step inside the machinery of medicine. In that short window, I am exposed to a lot. I have felt the silent weight of a family receiving news that their loved one is not coming home. I have stood beside a patient fresh from surgery, still searching for a hand to hold. I have sat with an anxious, angry patient who believed they were being held hostage — and I have learned that anger in a hospital bed is almost never about what it appears to be.

What I am learning, shift by shift, is that the difference between a doctor who survives and a doctor who transforms is not resilience in the way we usually mean it. It is one thing to pretend to be fine. It is something else to train the heart and brain to stay focused — and to radiate love through everything that unfolds.

Kevin was right. We will always need doctors. But we will always need doctors who stay in their hearts.

I do not yet know what specialty I will pursue. I do know the kind of doctor I am training to become. She will not pretend the chaos does not affect her. She will practice presence the way a surgeon practices suturing — deliberately, repeatedly, knowing mastery is a lifetime project, not a credential. She will remember that the angry patient is probably terrified, that the family receiving bad news needs someone who can sit in the silence, and that the volunteer at the edge of the trauma room is often the most important person in it — because she is still learning to see the whole person, not just the case.

I was that volunteer. I am still that volunteer. And I am applying to medical school not because I believe I am ready, but because I believe I am willing — willing to train my heart as rigorously as my mind, to stay present when presence is hardest to offer, and to become the doctor Kevin Crowe described on a yellow couch in Florida, in a room so hot it could break you, except it didn't. It made me.
